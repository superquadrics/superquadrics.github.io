Superquadric Primitives

Superquadrics are shapes described by a handful of parameters: three scales and two exponents that morph a shape continuously between boxes, cylinders, spheres and octahedra. Their extensions add tapering, bending and shearing (deformable superellipsoids), thin-walled containers (superbowls) and rings (supertoroids), so one compact parametrization covers most everyday shapes. Pick a family and drag the sliders.

    Composite Objects

    Most objects are not a single primitive but a few of them: a mug is a superbowl with a bent handle, a hammer two cylinders. Each object category is a template that places its primitives from a few object parameters (heights, radii, handle sizes, ...), so a whole category is covered by sampling these parameters within their ranges. The categories follow the DSQNet and TablewareNet datasets, plus custom ones.

        Primitive Grasps

        Since each primitive has a closed-form surface, grasp poses follow directly from its parameters rather than from a search over point clouds: every grasp is indexed by a few normalized coordinates on the shape, and its validity (the width fits the gripper, the contacts lie within the friction cone, the gripper does not collide) is checked analytically. Move the coordinates to slide the grasp over the surface.

          Object Grasps

          Valid grasps of a composite object are the grid grasps of all its primitives that fit the gripper (width and friction cone) and whose gripper does not collide with any part. Up to 15 diverse grasps are then chosen by farthest point sampling, with the distance between grasps \(|c_i - c_j| + 0.05\,\|R_i - R_j\|_F\) (grasp centers \(c\), orientations \(R\)).

          Grasp Simulation

          A MuJoCo simulation (WebAssembly) of the Franka FR3 (with the Franka hand) from MuJoCo Menagerie. The object is a free body made of convex pieces of its primitives. The grasps of the object (Franka hand, as above) are filtered against the floor, ordered from top-down to side approaches, and solved by inverse kinematics of the hand; the arm then follows pre-grasp → grasp → close → lift with its position actuators.

          Reactive Grasping

          The same simulation, controlled every frame from the current object pose. Drag the object with the mouse: the robot follows it at the pre-grasp pose of the best reachable grasp (re-selected as the object moves), and grasps and lifts it, also while it is being moved. If the gripper closes on nothing, it opens and tries again. Release lets the object go; the robot then waits until the object is moved before grasping it again.
